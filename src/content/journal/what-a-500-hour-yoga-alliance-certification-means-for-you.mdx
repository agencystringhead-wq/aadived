When you're choosing a yoga teacher, you'll often see letters after a name: RYT 200, RYT 500, E-RYT. They look official, but what do they actually tell you? And do they matter as much as how a teacher makes you feel in the room?

This guide explains what a 500-hour Yoga Alliance credential means, what it can and can't tell you, and the questions worth asking before you book with any teacher.

## Who Yoga Alliance is

Yoga Alliance is an international organisation that keeps a registry of yoga teachers and yoga schools. Teachers who complete a training with a Registered Yoga School (RYS) can apply to register with Yoga Alliance, and may then use the title Registered Yoga Teacher (RYT).

It's a registry, not a government licence, and registration is voluntary. Many students look for a credential as one sign of serious training.

## What "500-hour" means

The number refers to hours of teacher training. A 200-hour training is a common foundation, so a 500-hour designation indicates a longer period of study than that starting point. It usually involves deeper work on subjects such as anatomy, teaching methods, philosophy and how to adapt practice for different bodies.

On the Yoga Alliance site, the [certificate requirements for RYT registration](https://yogaalliance.org/policies-priorities-progress/certificate-requirements-for-ryt-registration/) explain that applicants provide proof of completing a training at a Registered Yoga School. The certificate must show the school's name, a signature from an authorised signatory, the training level (called the track designation) and the date of completion. If you'd like exact details of the 500-hour requirements, please check Yoga Alliance's own pages.

## What the credential can tell you

A 500-hour registration can be a helpful signal. In general, it suggests that a teacher:

- **Has completed a substantial amount of formal training.** That's a sign of commitment to the craft.
- **Has studied beyond the basics.** More hours often means more time on teaching methods, anatomy and adapting poses.
- **Trained with a recognised school.** A Registered Yoga School has met the registry's standards.
- **Can be checked.** Registration is something you can ask about and confirm, so you aren't relying on a claim alone.

## What it can't tell you

It's equally important to know the limits.

- **It doesn't guarantee a good teacher.** Skill in teaching, listening and adjusting is learned over time, and varies from person to person.
- **It doesn't tell you about their style.** One teacher may be dynamic and another quiet and restorative.
- **It doesn't replace medical knowledge.** Yoga teachers aren't doctors, and a credential doesn't mean they can diagnose or treat any condition.
- **It doesn't mean they suit your needs.** If you're pregnant, recovering from an injury or managing a health condition, you may need a teacher with particular experience.
- **It isn't the only route to good teaching.** Some excellent teachers are trained in other traditions or lineages.

## What may matter more than letters

Credentials are one signal among several. When you're choosing a teacher, you might also consider:

1. **How clearly they explain.** Do they tell you what to do and why, in plain words?
2. **How patient they are.** Do they let you go at your own pace?
3. **How they handle your questions.** Are they open, and honest about what they don't know?
4. **How they adapt.** Do they offer gentler options and use props?
5. **How they make you feel.** Do you feel safe and respected?
6. **Their experience with people like you.** If you're a beginner or have particular needs, ask.

## Questions to ask before you book

You're allowed to ask a teacher questions. Good teachers welcome them. A few to try:

1. **What training have you completed, and where?**
2. **Are you registered with Yoga Alliance, and at what level?**
3. **How long have you been teaching?**
4. **Have you worked with people who are pregnant, new to yoga or managing health conditions?**
5. **How do you adapt a class for different levels?**
6. **What should I expect in my first session?**
7. **What do you do if something hurts or I feel unwell?**

A confident teacher will be happy to answer.

## How to check a registration

If a teacher says they're registered, you can ask to see their certificate, and check Yoga Alliance's own website for how to look up a registered teacher or school. Look for the teacher's name, the level of registration and whether it's current. If you can't find them, ask the teacher. There may be a simple explanation, and a good teacher won't mind you asking.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a 500-hour teacher better than a 200-hour teacher?

Not necessarily. More hours of training can mean more depth, but a teacher's care, clarity and experience matter as well. Ask questions and try a class.

### Does Yoga Alliance certify yoga teachers by law?

No. Yoga Alliance is a registry, and registration is voluntary. It's a helpful signal, not a legal licence.

### Can a yoga teacher advise me on a medical condition?

Yoga teachers can adapt a practice, but they aren't doctors. Please speak to your doctor about any medical concern, and tell your teacher what your doctor advises.
