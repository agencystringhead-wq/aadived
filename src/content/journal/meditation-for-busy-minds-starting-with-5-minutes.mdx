You sit down to meditate, close your eyes, and within ten seconds you're thinking about an email, tonight's dinner and something you said in a meeting last week. So you decide you're bad at meditation and quit. If that sounds familiar, you've just had an ordinary first attempt.

Meditation isn't about emptying your head. It's about noticing where your attention has gone and gently bringing it back. That noticing is the whole practice, and you can start with five minutes.

## What meditation is, and what it isn't

Most people picture someone sitting perfectly still with a blank mind. That picture puts off many beginners before they've started. In practice, meditation is closer to training a habit: you pick one thing to rest your attention on, such as your breath, and each time your mind wanders off, you notice and return.

You don't need to stop thoughts. You need to stop following every one of them. A wandering mind isn't a sign you're doing it wrong. Noticing that it has wandered is the moment the practice happens.

Meditation isn't a religion you have to sign up to, and it isn't a replacement for medical care. It's a simple, quiet habit that many people find helps them feel steadier in a busy day.

## Why five minutes is enough to begin

A long sit can feel like a test you're bound to fail. Five minutes is short enough to fit before work, in a car park or after the school run, and long enough to notice how your mind behaves. Many beginners find that a short daily sit is easier to keep up than a long sit once a week.

The aim for the first few weeks isn't depth. It's showing up. If you manage five minutes on most days, you're building the habit that everything else rests on.

## Before you sit: four small choices

1. **Pick a time you already have.** Right after you wake, before you open your phone, or just after brushing your teeth works for many people. Attaching the sit to something you already do makes it easier to remember.
2. **Choose a spot.** A chair, a cushion or the edge of your bed is fine. Keep your back reasonably tall and your shoulders loose.
3. **Set a timer.** Use a soft alarm so you aren't checking the clock. Five minutes, no more for now.
4. **Silence the phone.** Put it on airplane mode or leave it in another room.

## A five-minute breath meditation

This is the simplest way in. Read it through once, then try it.

1. **Settle (30 seconds).** Sit comfortably, close your eyes or lower your gaze, and let your hands rest on your lap. Take two slow breaths.
2. **Find the breath (1 minute).** Notice where you feel it most clearly: the nostrils, the chest or the belly. Rest your attention there.
3. **Count (2 minutes).** Count each out-breath from one to ten, then start again at one. If you lose count, or you get to forty without noticing, just begin at one. Nothing has gone wrong.
4. **Let go of counting (1 minute).** Keep following the breath without numbers. Notice it rising and falling.
5. **Close (30 seconds).** Open your eyes slowly. Take a breath, look around the room, and notice how you feel before you pick up your phone.

## When your mind won't settle

Every beginner hits these. Here's what tends to help.

### "I can't stop thinking."

You're not meant to. When a thought pulls you away, quietly label it, "planning" or "worrying", and return to the next breath. You may have to do this a hundred times in five minutes. That's a hundred small repetitions, not a hundred failures.

### "I keep falling asleep."

Sit upright, open your eyes slightly, or try a time of day when you're more awake. Meditating lying down after dinner often leads to a nap rather than a sit.

### "I'm restless and my body itches."

Notice the itch without scratching straight away. If it stays, adjust your position slowly. A short walk or a few minutes of gentle stretches before you sit may help you settle.

### "I feel bored."

Boredom is part of it. You might try bringing a little curiosity to the breath: is it cool or warm, shallow or deep, quick or slow?

## Fitting it into a working day

If you work in Hinjawadi or anywhere with long hours and long commutes, you might not have a quiet morning. Some options:

- **Before your laptop opens.** Sit for five minutes before you check any messages.
- **After lunch.** Find a quiet corner, a meeting room or a bench outside, and do a short sit.
- **Between tasks.** A one-minute pause to take five slow breaths before a call.
- **At the end of the day.** Sit for five minutes before you leave your desk, to mark the shift from work to home.

## Growing the habit

After two or three weeks of five-minute sits, you can stretch to eight or ten minutes if it feels right. If it doesn't, five is still a perfectly good practice. Some people add a simple intention, such as "today I'll be patient," at the start, or end with a few moments of quiet before they stand.

Keep a note on your phone if it helps: the date and one word about how the sit felt. Over a few weeks you may spot patterns, like sitting more easily in the morning than late at night.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long until meditation feels easier?

It varies. Many people notice that coming back to the breath gets a little easier over a few weeks of regular practice, but there's no set timeline. Focus on sitting regularly rather than on results.

### Should I meditate with my eyes open or closed?

Either is fine. Closing your eyes cuts down distractions, while a soft, lowered gaze can help if you tend to feel sleepy or uneasy with your eyes shut.

### Do I need a teacher or an app?

You can start alone with the five-minute routine above. A teacher can answer questions, correct your posture and offer practices that suit you, which can be helpful if you feel stuck.

You don't need a quiet life to begin, only a few spare minutes. Sit down, breathe, and start again whenever you wander.
