If you've been told you have PCOS, or you suspect you might, you've probably read a lot of advice, some of it conflicting. One thing most guidance agrees on is that regular, gentle movement is a good habit to have. Yoga is one way to build it, and many women find it a kind, low-pressure place to start.

This article explains how gentle yoga may support you alongside your doctor's care, offers a calm routine and points out what to go easy on. It won't claim that yoga is a medical answer to PCOS, because it isn't.

<Note title="Before you read">

This article is for general information. Please speak to your doctor before starting a new practice, especially if you're pregnant or managing a health condition. Yoga doesn't replace medical care for PCOS.

</Note>

## What PCOS is, briefly

PCOS, which stands for polycystic ovary syndrome, is a common hormone condition. The NHS now also calls it polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS). According to the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/polycystic-ovary-syndrome-pcos/), it can affect hair growth, periods, fertility and mood. There's no cure, but treatments and lifestyle changes can help.

Common symptoms listed by the NHS include irregular or infrequent periods, excess hair growth or hair loss, weight gain, difficulty getting pregnant, low mood or anxiety, acne and tiredness. Symptoms can have many causes, so if you think you might have PCOS, or your symptoms affect your daily life, the NHS advises seeing a doctor.

For lifestyle, the NHS suggests a healthy, balanced diet and regular exercise. That's where gentle yoga can fit in, as one form of regular movement among others you might enjoy.

## How gentle yoga may support you

Yoga isn't medical care, but it may support the habits and feelings that surround living with PCOS.

- **Regular movement.** A short practice, done often, is easier to keep up than an intense workout you dread.
- **Stress and mood.** Slow breathing and quiet time can help you feel calmer, which many people find supports their wellbeing.
- **Body awareness.** Tuning in to how you feel on different days can help you notice patterns, and give you useful notes for your doctor.
- **Kindness to your body.** A gentle practice can be a way of caring for your body, rather than pushing it.
- **Rest.** Restorative poses give your body a chance to slow down, which many people don't allow themselves.

<PullQuote>With PCOS, gentle and regular usually beats hard and occasional.</PullQuote>

## What to go easy on

You don't have to avoid much, but a few cautions are sensible.

- **Over-exertion.** Pushing yourself to exhaustion adds stress to your body. Aim to finish feeling better than when you started.
- **Comparison.** Your practice is yours. Skip comparing yourself with classmates or social media.
- **Holding your breath or straining.** Keep your breathing easy in every pose.
- **Pain.** Stop anything that causes sharp or lasting pain.
- **Heated rooms,** if you tend to feel dizzy or drained. Ask your doctor if you're unsure.

If you have another health condition, such as high blood pressure or joint pain, tell your teacher so they can adapt the practice.

## A gentle 20-minute routine

Use a mat, a cushion and a wall. Move slowly, and rest between poses whenever you like.

1. **Easy seated breathing, 2 minutes.** Breathe in for four, out for six. Let your shoulders drop.
2. **Cat-cow, 2 minutes.** On hands and knees, move your spine slowly with your breath.
3. **Child's pose with knees wide, 2 minutes.** Rest your forehead on a cushion.
4. **Low lunge, 1 minute each side.** Step one foot forward, lower the back knee on a folded blanket, and lift your chest gently.
5. **Seated forward fold, 2 minutes.** Sit with your legs out and knees soft. Fold over a cushion on your thighs, keeping your back long.
6. **Supported bridge, 2 minutes.** Lie on your back with knees bent. Lift your hips and rest a block or cushion under your sacrum.
7. **Seated butterfly, 2 minutes.** Bring the soles of your feet together and rest your hands on your feet, leaning gently forward.
8. **Reclined twist, 1 minute each side.** Lie on your back, let both knees fall to one side, and keep your shoulders soft.
9. **Legs up the wall, 3 minutes.** Rest your legs against a wall and close your eyes.

Finish with a minute or two lying on your back. Place a hand on your belly and feel it rise and fall.

## How often, and for how long

There's no perfect schedule. A few ideas:

- **Start small.** Two or three sessions a week of ten to twenty minutes is a good beginning.
- **Build slowly.** Add a session or a few minutes when it feels natural.
- **Walk too.** Short walks count as movement, and sit well alongside yoga.
- **Rest days are fine.** Your body needs time to recover, especially if you feel tired.
- **Track gently.** A simple note on how you felt can help you see what suits you.

## Working with your cycle

If your periods are irregular, planning by cycle phase can be tricky, so there's no need to follow a strict plan. Instead, tune in to how you feel on the day:

- **Low energy:** choose restorative poses and longer rests.
- **Steady energy:** a standing sequence with a few extra breaths in each pose is fine.
- **Heavy bleeding or pain:** go gentle, and avoid strong inversions. Speak to your doctor if the bleeding is heavy or the pain is severe.

## When to speak to your doctor

Please see your doctor if you notice:

- Periods that stop, become very irregular or are very heavy
- Severe or persistent pelvic pain
- Signs of low mood or anxiety that affect your daily life
- New or worsening symptoms
- Difficulty getting pregnant, if that's something you're hoping for

Yoga should sit alongside, never in place of, your doctor's advice and any treatment you've been prescribed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is yoga a replacement for PCOS care?

No. According to the NHS, there's no cure for PCOS, but treatments and lifestyle changes can help. Yoga may support your wellbeing as part of a wider plan agreed with your doctor.

### Is a gentle practice enough?

Many people find that gentle, regular movement is a good place to begin. What's right for you depends on your health, so ask your doctor how much and what kind of exercise suits you.

### Should I avoid yoga during my period?

Not necessarily. Many people practise through their period at a gentler pace. Avoid strong inversions and intense core work if they feel uncomfortable, and rest if you need to.

Be kind to yourself. Gentle practice is a way of showing up for your body, and that's worth doing regularly.
