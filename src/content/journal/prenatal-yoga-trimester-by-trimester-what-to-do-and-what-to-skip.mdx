Pregnancy changes how your body feels almost week by week, so a practice that suits you in the first few weeks may not suit you a few months later. This guide walks through each trimester: gentle practices many prenatal teachers use, what to leave out, and when to stop. It's written for expecting mothers, including the many new parents juggling long days in Hinjawadi's offices and a body that's busy doing something extraordinary.

<Note title="Before you read">

This article is for general information. Please speak to your doctor before starting a new practice, especially if you're pregnant or managing a health condition.

</Note>

## Start with your doctor

Yoga can be a calm, supportive part of pregnancy for many people, but whether it's right for you depends on your own health. Before your first session, ask your doctor whether any kind of movement is off limits for you, and ask about anything particular to your pregnancy, such as blood pressure, the position of the placenta or a previous complication.

Then tell your teacher you're pregnant and how many weeks along you are. A prenatal class or a one-on-one session is usually a better fit than a general class, because the practice can be adjusted for you. Whichever you choose, three rules hold in every trimester:

1. **Move slowly, and get up slowly.** Rising quickly from the floor may make you feel dizzy, so come up in stages.
2. **Keep your breathing easy.** Breathe in and out through your nose. If you can't hold a conversation, ease off.
3. **Stop anything that hurts.** Discomfort that doesn't settle, or any sharp or pulling sensation, is your cue to come out of the pose.

## First trimester: weeks 1 to 12

Tiredness and nausea are common in these weeks, and the bump hasn't appeared yet, so it's tempting either to push on as usual or to stop altogether. A short, gentle practice often sits in the middle. Aim for ten to fifteen minutes on the days you feel up to it, and rest on the days you don't.

**Practices many people find comfortable**

- **Cat-cow:** six to eight slow breaths on hands and knees, moving the spine gently.
- **Supported child's pose with the knees wide:** this leaves room for your belly. Rest your forehead on a cushion and take five slow breaths.
- **Seated side stretch:** sit comfortably and reach one arm overhead, then the other, for three breaths each side.
- **Easy seated breathing:** breathe in for a count of four and out for a count of six, for one to two minutes. Longer out-breaths may help you feel calmer.

**What to leave out**

- Hot or heated rooms, because overheating isn't recommended in pregnancy.
- Strong core work, and holding your breath.
- Deep, closed twists, where you wrap one elbow across your knee.

## Second trimester: weeks 13 to 27

Energy often returns in the middle of pregnancy, and many people feel ready to practise a little more. This is a good time to build gentle strength and steadiness, with support close by.

**Practices many people find comfortable**

- **Wide-legged forward fold with your hands on a chair or wall:** keep your back long and your knees soft.
- **Warrior II near a wall:** a wall within reach gives you something to steady yourself against.
- **Cat-cow:** many people find this eases a tired lower back.
- **Side-lying rest:** lie on your side with a pillow between your knees and one under your head.

<PullQuote>Prenatal practice isn't about doing more. It's about feeling more at ease in a body that's changing.</PullQuote>

**What to leave out**

- Lying flat on your back. After the first trimester, please don't do this without medical advice. Many teachers use side-lying or propped-up positions instead, so ask your doctor what suits you.
- Inversions, such as headstand, shoulderstand and handstand.
- Deep twists. If you twist at all, keep it gentle, from the upper back, and open the chest away from the belly.
- Strong backbends and jumping.
- Balance poses away from a wall.

## Third trimester: week 28 to birth

As your bump grows, comfort and rest matter most. Sessions are often shorter and slower, with more time spent breathing and resting than moving. Your doctor or midwife may have particular advice for these weeks, so check in before you practise.

**Practices many people find comfortable**

- **Hands-and-knees rocking and cat-cow:** many people find this feels good for a tired back.
- **A supported squat:** sit on a block or a rolled blanket to open the hips, if your doctor is happy for you to do so.
- **Wall-supported stretches** for the chest and shoulders.
- **Side-lying rest,** with pillows wherever you need them.
- **Long out-breaths:** breathe in for four and out for six or more, to help you feel at ease.

**What to leave out**

- Lying on your back.
- Deep twists and inversions.
- Deep forward folds that press on the belly.
- Long standing holds that leave you light-headed.
- Moving fast, or pushing for a deeper stretch than feels comfortable.

## What to skip in every trimester

Here's the short list to keep in your head, whichever stage you're at.

- **Inversions:** headstand, shoulderstand and handstand.
- **Deep or closed twists.**
- **Deep backbends.**
- **Lying flat on your back after the first trimester,** unless your doctor says it's fine.
- **Holding your breath.**
- **Hot or heated rooms.**
- **Overstretching.** Pregnancy hormones may make your joints feel looser than usual, so go for steady, not deep.
- **Jumping and fast flows.**
- **Belly-down poses** once your bump grows.

## A gentle ten-minute routine

If your doctor has cleared you for gentle movement and you're in your second trimester, this short routine is a calm place to begin. Keep a chair, a wall and a cushion close by.

1. **Easy seated breathing, one minute.** In for four, out for six.
2. **Cat-cow, six breaths.** Slow and small.
3. **Supported child's pose with knees wide, five breaths.** Rest on a cushion or bolster.
4. **Wide-legged forward fold with hands on a chair, five breaths.** Knees soft.
5. **Warrior II at a wall, four breaths each side.** Keep your front knee over your ankle.
6. **Side-lying rest, two minutes.** Pillows under your head and between your knees, and let your breath settle.

## Signs to stop and speak to your doctor

Stop and contact your doctor if you notice any of the following during or after practice:

- Dizziness or feeling faint
- Shortness of breath that doesn't settle
- Chest pain
- A bad headache
- Vaginal bleeding or fluid leaking
- Regular, painful contractions
- Calf pain or swelling
- Less movement from your baby than usual

If something feels wrong, trust that feeling and call, even if it turns out to be nothing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I start yoga if I've never practised before?

Many people begin yoga during pregnancy. Speak to your doctor first, then choose a gentle prenatal class or a one-on-one session, so the practice can be explained and adjusted for you.

### Is it safe to practise in the first trimester?

Gentle movement is often fine in a low-risk pregnancy, but this depends on your own health and history. Ask your doctor, and tell your teacher how far along you are.

### How often should I practise?

Short, regular sessions are often easier to keep up than long ones. Your doctor can advise how much suits you, and it's fine for that to change from week to week.

You know your body best, and so does your doctor. Take it gently, and come back to the breath whenever you need to.
